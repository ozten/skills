# Comic Strip Story Translator

Transform any narrative material into compressed, visual 1-4 panel comic strips.

## Core Principle

A comic strip is a **highlight reel, not a documentary**. You're extracting the most potent instant, not adapting the whole story.

## Essential Constraints

Before translating any story, internalize these limits:

| Constraint | Limit | Why It Matters |
|------------|-------|----------------|
| Words per panel | 25 maximum (10 or fewer for punchlines) | Readers spend seconds on a strip. Crowded panels kill momentum. |
| Words per strip | 50–80 total | Economy is essential for short-form storytelling. |
| Panels | 1–4 (typically 3–4) | Each panel is one frozen moment. More panels = slower pacing. |
| Dialogue per balloon | 3 lines maximum | Longer balloons obscure the art and slow reading. |

**The First-Reader Test:** Every strip must work for someone who has never seen your characters before. If a new reader can't understand and enjoy the strip, it needs revision.

## Workflow

### Step 1: Identify the Core Moment

Ask: **What is the single most visual, emotionally resonant, or funny moment in this material?**

**Finding strategies by genre:**
- **Comedy:** Find the punchline first, then work backward
- **Drama:** Find the turning point or emotional peak
- **Slice-of-life:** Find the moment of recognition or surprise

The entire strip exists to deliver this moment.

### Step 2: Determine the Minimum Context

What does the reader *absolutely need* to understand that core moment? Strip away everything else.

**Cut ruthlessly:**
- Backstory the art can imply (setting, relationships, mood)
- Transitions between locations
- Actions the reader can infer through "closure"

**Closure** is the reader's mental participation—they fill gaps between panels. Show a character winding up a punch in panel 1 and lying on the ground in panel 2; the reader constructs the fight.

### Step 3: Map to Panel Structure

Assign each essential beat to a panel using the four-panel structure:

```
PANEL 1: [Context] What situation are we in?
PANEL 2: [Development] What does the character want or do?
PANEL 3: [Complication] What goes wrong or shifts?
PANEL 4: [Resolution] What's the payoff?
```

**Variations:**
- **4 panels** = Full three-act structure with build
- **3 panels** = Compress panels 2 and 3 into a single beat
- **2 panels** = Setup → punchline with no build
- **1 panel (gag cartoon)** = Entire joke in a single frozen moment

**If you have more than 4 beats, you have too much story.** Combine beats or split into multiple strips.

### Step 4: Apply the Compression Test

For each panel, ask:
1. Does this panel advance the story or joke?
2. Can this information be shown in the art instead of told in dialogue?
3. Would cutting this panel break the strip?

If a panel fails these tests, merge it with another or delete it.

### Step 5: Write the Same Strip in Fewer Panels

As a discipline exercise, rewrite your strip with one fewer panel. Then do it again.

- 4-panel strip → 3 panels
- 3-panel strip → 2 panels
- 2-panel strip → 1 panel

This reveals what's truly essential. Sometimes the shorter version is stronger.

## Alternative Structure: Kishotenketsu

The Japanese four-part structure offers an alternative to Western conflict-driven narrative:

1. **Introduction** — Establish the scene
2. **Development** — Continue or elaborate
3. **Twist** — Introduce something unexpected
4. **Conclusion** — Resolve with new perspective

This allows stories without conflict by substituting *surprise* for antagonism.

## Output Format

After translating, provide:

1. **Panel count and structure** — Which format you chose and why
2. **Core moment identification** — What instant the strip delivers
3. **What was cut** — Key elements from the source that didn't make it and why
4. **Panel-by-panel breakdown** — Brief description of each panel's function
5. **Word count verification** — Confirm under 80 words total

## Common Translation Pitfalls

| Pitfall | What Goes Wrong | The Fix |
|---------|-----------------|---------|
| **Cramming** | Too much story in too few panels | One frozen moment per panel; trust closure |
| **Burying the lead** | Setup takes too long; payoff arrives late | Get to the core moment fast; hook readers in panel 1 |
| **Weak premise** | Joke requires too much context to understand | Apply the first-reader test; simplify setup |
| **Missing visual potential** | Selecting a moment that's dialogue-heavy | Choose moments with strong visual or emotional components |

## Quick Reference

**Panel Structure Template:**
```
PANEL 1: Context (who, where, when)
PANEL 2: Goal (what does the character want?)
PANEL 3: Conflict (what goes wrong?)
PANEL 4: Payoff (resolution or punchline)
```

**Before moving forward:**
1. Does it work for a first-time reader?
2. Can I tell this in one fewer panel?
3. Is the core moment worth the space it takes?
4. Am I showing instead of telling?
