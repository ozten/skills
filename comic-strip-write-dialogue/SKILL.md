# Comic Strip Dialogue Writer

Write compressed, character-specific dialogue that elevates the visual storytelling.

## Core Principle

Strip dialogue is not conversation—it's **compressed speech**. Every word must carry weight.

## Essential Constraints

| Constraint | Limit | Why It Matters |
|------------|-------|----------------|
| Words per panel | 25 maximum | More text crowds the art and slows reading |
| Punchline | 10 words or fewer | The payoff must land cleanly and quickly |
| Lines per balloon | 3 maximum | Longer balloons obscure art and break reading flow |
| Words per strip | 50–80 total | Economy is the soul of the strip format |

## Workflow

### Step 1: Write Naturally, Then Cut

Draft the dialogue as your characters would actually speak, including all verbal filler and natural rhythm. **Then cut 50% of the words.**

**Example transformation:**

**Before (conversational):**
> "Hey, I was thinking that maybe we should probably go and get some food because I'm starting to feel kind of hungry, you know?"

**After (compressed):**
> "I'm hungry. Food?"

The second version preserves intent while respecting the format.

### Step 2: Apply the Character Voice Test

Cover the character names. **Can you tell who's speaking from the dialogue alone?**

Each character needs:
- **Distinct vocabulary** — Formal vs. casual, technical vs. simple
- **Consistent speech rhythm** — Short sentences vs. long, questions vs. statements
- **Signature phrases** — Used sparingly, not in every strip

**Voice differentiation examples:**

| Character | Voice Pattern | Example |
|-----------|---------------|---------|
| Professor | Formal, precise, technical terms | "Fascinating. The mechanism suggests..." |
| Street kid | Casual, slang, sentence fragments | "Yo. That's wild." |
| Robot | Literal, no contractions, systematic | "I do not understand the reference." |
| Boss | Imperatives, business jargon | "Make it happen. I need ROI by Friday." |

**If any character's dialogue could be swapped with another's, rewrite it.**

### Step 3: Read Aloud

Speak every line out loud. **If you stumble, the reader will stumble.**

Listen for:
- Unnatural phrasing
- Too many syllables
- Missing contractions (use "don't" not "do not")
- Tongue-twisters or awkward consonant clusters
- Lines that don't sound like how people actually talk

Record yourself if needed. Natural speech has rhythm; stilted dialogue lacks it.

### Step 4: Let the Art Carry Emotion

**Never write dialogue that describes what the art shows.** This is "double writing"—wasting space and insulting the reader.

| Don't Write | Do Write |
|-------------|----------|
| "I'm so angry right now!" | [Character's expression shows anger] |
| "Look, a dragon is attacking the castle!" | "Run!" [Art shows dragon] |
| "I feel sad because my dog died." | [Character sits alone, empty leash in hand] |
| "I'm jumping out the window!" | [Art shows the action, character silent or says: "Here goes..."] |

**If the panel shows a character crying, you don't need a caption saying "She was devastated."**

The script and art should complement each other, not repeat each other.

### Step 5: Punchline Placement

For comedy strips, the punchline goes in the **final panel, final balloon, final sentence**.

Front-load setup; end on the laugh.

**Punchline requirements:**
- 10 words or fewer
- A single beat (no "and then...")
- The last thing the reader sees before moving on
- Surprising yet inevitable

**Example structures:**

**Weak punchline placement:**
```
PANEL 3: "That's why I never trust escalators."
PANEL 4: "Yeah, they're always up to something. Anyway, let's go."
```
*The joke lands in panel 3, then dies in panel 4*

**Strong punchline placement:**
```
PANEL 3: "Why don't you trust escalators?"
PANEL 4: "They're always up to something."
```
*The strip ends on the laugh*

## Dialogue Patterns to Avoid

| Pattern | Why It's Wrong | Fix |
|---------|----------------|-----|
| **Exposition dumps** | Explaining backstory or world-building through dialogue | Show through art; trust context |
| **On-the-nose emotion** | "I'm so happy/sad/angry!" | Let facial expressions and body language convey |
| **Unnecessary names** | "John, let's go." "Okay, Sarah." | People rarely use names in conversation |
| **Explaining the joke** | Adding dialogue after the punchline | End on the laugh; silence is powerful |
| **Microphone testing** | "So, as I was saying earlier..." | Get to the point immediately |

## Character Voice Checklist

Before finalizing dialogue, verify:

- [ ] Each character has distinct vocabulary and rhythm?
- [ ] Dialogue sounds natural when read aloud?
- [ ] Contractions used appropriately (except for formal/robotic characters)?
- [ ] No character sounds like you, the writer?
- [ ] Dialogue could not be swapped between characters without losing meaning?

## Silence is Dialogue

Not every panel needs words. Strategic silence can be:
- More powerful than exposition
- A beat for comedic timing
- Space for the art to breathe
- The punchline itself

**When to use silent panels:**
- Emotional moments where expressions speak louder
- Visual gags that need no explanation
- The beat before a punchline
- Reaction shots

## Output Format

When completing dialogue work, verify:

1. **Word count** — Total under 80, no panel over 25, punchline under 10
2. **Voice distinction** — Each character sounds unique
3. **Read-aloud test** — All dialogue flows naturally
4. **Art-dialogue balance** — No double writing, emotion shown not told
5. **Punchline placement** — Final panel, final balloon, final sentence

## Quick Reference

**Compression Process:**
1. Write conversationally
2. Cut 50% of words
3. Remove redundancy with art
4. Verify each character's distinct voice
5. Read aloud for natural flow

**Before submitting:**
- Cover character names—can you still tell who's speaking?
- Read aloud—do you stumble anywhere?
- Check art descriptions—does dialogue duplicate visual information?
- Verify punchline lands last
